Chapter 3 — Multiple Qubits, Tensor Products, and Entanglement

Chapter purpose. Build enough conceptual and mathematical understanding to predict an experiment before running it. The projects below are applications of the chapter, not substitutes for the theory.

1. The multi-qubit state space

Two qubits require four basis states: |00⟩, |01⟩, |10⟩, and |11⟩. In general n qubits have 2^n computational-basis amplitudes. This exponential state-space growth is important, but it should not be confused with an automatic exponential computational speedup.

2. Tensor products

Independent subsystem descriptions combine through the tensor product. If |a⟩=a₀|0⟩+a₁|1⟩ and |b⟩=b₀|0⟩+b₁|1⟩, then |a⟩⊗|b⟩ contains amplitudes a₀b₀, a₀b₁, a₁b₀, and a₁b₁ for the four basis states. Tensor products also combine operators acting on different qubits.

3. Controlled operations

A CNOT uses one qubit as control and conditionally flips a target. Its importance is not that it resembles an if-statement; together with suitable single-qubit gates it can create correlations that cannot be represented as independent single-qubit states.

4. Bell states and entanglement

The Bell state |Φ+⟩=(|00⟩+|11⟩)/√2 cannot be factored into |a⟩⊗|b⟩. Each individual computational-basis result is random, but joint results are correlated. Entanglement is therefore a property of the joint quantum state, not merely a claim that two classical variables happen to agree.

5. GHZ states

The n-qubit GHZ family (|00…0⟩+|11…1⟩)/√2 extends multipartite correlation. GHZ experiments are useful for teaching state preparation, scaling, circuit depth, connectivity, and the increasing fragility of larger entangled states on noisy hardware.

Student exploration and activities

Use cases: teleportation, networking, and correlated quantum information

Entanglement supports protocols such as teleportation and superdense coding and is a central resource in quantum information. In this course, Bell and GHZ states also serve as controlled experiments for learning multi-qubit state spaces, correlation, scaling, and noise sensitivity.

Problem: correlation is not automatically entanglement

Two classical variables can be perfectly correlated. Therefore 'we measured the same bit' is not, by itself, a complete explanation of entanglement. Students should learn what the joint state is, why it cannot be factored, and why basis choice matters.

Suggested student activity

Students can compare independent fair bits, a classically correlated mixture, and an entangled Bell state. They can investigate which measurements distinguish the cases and why additional measurement bases matter.

Questions for exploration

Students can make a prediction, explain the reasoning in plain language, and identify an observation that would challenge the prediction. The outcome can then be connected to the mathematical model introduced in this chapter.

Worked example

Start with |00⟩. Apply H to qubit 0: (|00⟩+|10⟩)/√2. CNOT with qubit 0 controlling qubit 1 maps |00⟩→|00⟩ and |10⟩→|11⟩. The final state is |Φ+⟩. Ideal computational-basis measurement therefore produces only 00 and 11, each with probability 1/2.

How to reason about the result

Separate three layers: (1) the ideal mathematical prediction, (2) finite-shot sampling, and (3) implementation effects such as noise or compilation. A good laboratory report identifies which layer can plausibly explain an observed discrepancy.

Check your understanding

  1. Why is (|00⟩+|01⟩)/√2 not entangled? Factor it.
  2. What is the dimension of the state vector for five qubits?
  3. Why do matching measurement outcomes alone not constitute a complete experimental demonstration of all properties of entanglement?
